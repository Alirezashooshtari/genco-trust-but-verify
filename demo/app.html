GENCO proposes a state. Newton-Raphson decides.

GENCO predicts every voltage and angle of a contingency in one forward pass. On its own it returns a plausible grid for every case, including cases past voltage collapse. The verifier turns that prediction into a starting point and lets the AC equations make the call.

The workflow

1. GENCO

One forward pass on a T4 GPU (about 70 ms here) gives the state of all 2,000 buses.

2. Warning

A logistic regression on GENCO's outputs and embeddings scores how likely the solver is to fail. It orders the queue; it decides nothing.

3. Newton-Raphson

Starts from GENCO's state; if that fails, from the pre-contingency, DC and flat states. Converged means a power mismatch below 10−8 p.u.

4. Outcome

Verified safe: an AC solution with no limit violated
Verified violation: an overload or a voltage outside 0.9 to 1.1 p.u.
Unresolved: no start converged; the case goes to an engineer

What the outcomes mean, and what they do not

  • Unresolved is a statement about the solver and its four starts, not a proof that no AC solution exists. In the storms studied, continuation confirmed that the solution folds below the actual load.
  • The equations can have two solutions. In 2 of the 748 verified cases GENCO's start reached a high-voltage solution and the classical starts a low-voltage one. A static power flow cannot say which the grid would settle on.
  • The warning catches 95 % of unresolved cases at its preset threshold, with 16 % false alarms, and is close to chance right at a voltage-collapse point. The solver still runs on every case.
  • On this grid GENCO does not make screening faster: its start saves about one Newton iteration, but inference costs more than the classical solve.
  • One grid, one fine-tuning run, research code. Not for operational use.
